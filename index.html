Kazakh textbook

Cover art and page design for a Kazakh language textbook (work in progress).

Cover art

The illustration references multiple mountain regions and a lake in Kazakhstan, combined into one abstract landscape so diverse geographic features are represented in a single image.

The stylized approach lets the cover feel cohesive rather than region-specific, while still evoking a strong sense of place. The goal was a striking, inviting cover that captures attention and encourages readers to pick up the book and begin studying the language.

Page design

I focused on visual clarity, engagement, and usability.

Cohesion across the spread. The blue header shape visually crosses page boundaries when facing pages align.

Interactive elements. QR codes link to supplemental content such as audio recordings, videos, and online textbook materials.

Color. The scheme draws from the Kazakh flag, using contrasting hues from opposite ends of the spectrum for clarity and emphasis.

Contextual learning. Enriching, non-essential vocabulary is defined in the bottom margins so it supports learners without distracting from core content.

Club logos

Two University of Washington club identities, each built around a symbol its members already recognize.

Husky Cycling Club

I designed this logo to capture the ethos of the University of Washington Husky Cycling Club through a cartoon husky, the official mascot of UW and the club.

“UW” and “HCC” appear on the left and right paws, reading as University of Washington Husky Cycling Club. Lightning bolts represent speed and energy, and the fur pattern forms a subtle “W” for Washington. Aerodynamic sunglasses reference competitive cycling.

The purple and gold scheme reflects UW identity, while darker accents add contrast.

The team responded well, and the logo now appears on cycling jerseys, stickers, helmets, bicycles, and alumni materials.

Undergraduate Linguistics Club seal

The central “Wug,” an endearing symbol from a foundational study on language acquisition, anchors the design and reflects the club’s academic roots.

The surrounding network is bold and graphic, and can be read as either a neural network or a social network. It points to subfields such as Computational Linguistics, Sociolinguistics, and Language Acquisition.

A white foreground contrasts with UW’s signature purple background, and colorful nodes add pops of color.

Data presentation for nanotechnology research

Visualization and poster work from my internship at the Center for Integrated Nanotechnologies, Los Alamos National Laboratory.

Trajectories of antigens

This figure shows multiple three-dimensional trajectories of quantum-dot-labeled antigens undergoing random-walk motion in physiological solution, used to study antibody–antigen kinetics.

I wrote a Python visualization pipeline that reproduces the original color scheme from legacy C++ code. I defined an array of the seven required RGB values and implemented gradient interpolation by specifying the number of points per color, giving smooth transitions along each trajectory.

This preserved visual continuity and scientific accuracy while making complex 3D motion easy to interpret.

Student symposium poster

I designed this poster to describe my internship project: tracking the trajectories of quantum-dot-labeled antigens in physiological solution to predict antibody–antigen dissociation dynamics.

It highlights my Python-based graphical representation of three-dimensional trajectories and my calculations of antigen diffusivities using the Stokes–Einstein equation. The design emphasizes clarity and accessibility while accurately conveying complex kinetic data.

This work has been submitted for peer review and is expected to be published in Review of Scientific Instruments in 2026.

Designing a lab protocol people can repeat

Summer 2026, Center for Integrated Nanotechnologies, Los Alamos National Laboratory. I treated a bench protocol like a product: the users are other researchers, and the goal is that anyone can follow it and get the same result.

Making Safer Radiations Accessible for Cancer Treatments

Many cancer therapies harm healthy tissue, especially when treating metastatic disease. Targeted radionuclide therapy pairs power (radiation that acts locally) with precision (antibodies that carry it to tumor cells). I built the foundation: a reliable way to attach the trastuzumab antibody, which targets HER2-positive breast cancer cells, to gold nanoparticles.

The poster shows the same problem three ways: a motivation panel for newcomers, a chemistry diagram of how the antibody attaches, and a looping protocol cycle that a lab member can follow step by step.

  • 99% / 1%PEG-methoxy to PEG-DBCO gave the most stable particles
  • Copper-freeclick chemistry keeps the antibody able to find its target

The design loop

The protocol is a cycle I ran repeatedly, changing one variable at a time.

  1. PlanSet independent variables such as temperature, materials, ratios, time, and speed.
  2. BuildCoat gold nanoparticles with PEG and attach the SH-PEG-DBCO linker.
  3. TestCheck stability, recovery, and aggregation. UV-Vis confirms the DBCO modification worked.
  4. AnalyzeCompare how methoxy:DBCO ratio, Tween 20 percentage, and LoBind tubes affect results.
  5. RefineAdjust the PEG ratio and Tween 20, then improve the workflow.

What testing told me

Fewer lost particles

LoBind tubes reduced nanoparticles sticking to tube walls, improving recovery across the workflow.

Less clumping

Adding Tween 20 minimized aggregation and kept particles dispersed. The highest safe concentration still needs study so it doesn’t raise cell toxicity.

Right balance of sites

A mostly inert PEG coating with a little DBCO cut nonspecific interactions while leaving enough sites to attach antibodies.

My contributions

  • Synthesized gold-palladium core-shell nanoparticles to support research on safer, targeted cancer radiation therapies.
  • Developed a protocol for bioconjugating azide-tagged antibodies to DBCO-functionalized gold nanoparticles, establishing a reproducible platform for future work with radiolabeled 103Pd.
  • Conducted cytotoxicity assays and evaluated experimental results to inform subsequent design and testing decisions.
